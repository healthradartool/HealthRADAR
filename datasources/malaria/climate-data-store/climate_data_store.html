About the data portal

The Climate Data Store (CDS), is a comprehensive and open access platform operated by European Centre for Medium-Range Forecasting (ECMWF) for the Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S).

CDS is a Climate Data Repository providing access to a wide range of datasets including Observations (satellite and in-situ), Climate Reanalyses, Seasonal Forecasts, Climate Projections and Climate Indices. For each dataset, CDS provides an overview, download and documentation pages.

The site provides Training material to facilitate the use of datasets on the CDS. These include practical data processing workflows based on Jupyter notebooks; e-learning modules on key concepts behind the climate data types as well as learning resources from training events.

The site uses, and provides access to, earthkit which is a new open-source Python project developed by ECMWF that offers robust tools to accelerate workflows in weather and climate science. It streamlines data access, processing, analysis and visualization. At the time of publishing, earthkit is still in the BETA stage of development.

The CDS hosts a number of Applications which allow users to explore and visualize the historic, present and future climate.

Accessing the data portal

The Climate Data Store can be accessed via the following url - https://climate.copernicus.eu/climate-data-store

Users need to register or login to access many of the features on the climate Data Store (the login tab is found in the top right corner of the page). The CDS utilizes ECMWF user account which enables users to access the CDS as well as other ECMWF resources, events and courses. On CDS the user credentials are primarily used to track user activity, manage data access and ensure compliance with data licenses.

Access to the datasets is provided both interactively (manually over the web) or programmatically through an API service. The CDS provides an extensive User guide section which includes; CDS Documentation which provides users with a deeper understanding of what the Climate Data Store is and how best to use it; guidance on how to download data interactively via the CDS website and also how to get data programmatically via an API service.

What datasets are available

The CDS provides a catalogue which lists available datasets. Users can filter this extensive list of datasets through faceted or text-based searches. The site hosts a number of European-focused datasets, so it’s helpful to filter by Spatial coverage to identify those datasets with Global coverage as these would include the southern Africa domain.

The datasets can also be filtered by Product type. The definitions of the types is provided below since they may not be easily understandable to a non-climate audience

  • Climate indices: Quantitative measures derived from climate variables (e.g., temperature, precipitation) that capture key aspects of climate variability and change. Examples include the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) index and the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI).

  • Climate projections: Simulated future climate conditions based on climate models that use different greenhouse gas emission scenarios. These projections provide insights into potential long-term changes in climate but are subject to uncertainties related to model assumptions and future human activities.

  • Derived reanalysis: Climate datasets produced by applying additional calculations or transformations to primary reanalysis data. These may include indices, bias-corrected fields, or interpolated datasets that enhance usability for specific applications.

  • In-situ observations: Climate data collected directly at a specific location using ground-based instruments, such as weather stations, buoys, and radiosondes. These measurements provide high-accuracy climate information but have limited spatial coverage.

  • Reanalysis: A comprehensive dataset generated by combining historical climate observations with numerical weather models to reconstruct past climate conditions. Reanalysis datasets provide globally consistent and gridded climate information, widely used in climate research.

  • Satellite observations: Climate data collected by remote sensing instruments aboard satellites, covering various atmospheric, oceanic, and land-surface parameters. These observations provide global coverage and long-term records of climate variables such as temperature, precipitation, and sea surface height.

  • Seasonal forecasts: Predictions of climate conditions (e.g., temperature, precipitation) for upcoming months to a year, based on climate models and historical data. These forecasts help anticipate climate variations such as droughts, monsoons, and extreme weather events.

Some key datasets that may be of interest are:

Satellite observations

  • Precipitation monthly and daily gridded data from 1979 to present derived from satellite measurements The Global Precipitation Climatology Project (GPCP) provides global estimates of precipitation as monthly means and daily means, based on estimates using microwave and infrared satellite imagery. The dataset utilizes advanced data processing and calibration techniques to ensure accuracy and constancy of data.

Reanalysis

  • ERA5 hourly data on single levels from 1940 to present The ERA5 reanalysis outputs are grouped into datasets or catalogue entries. This dataset includes key variables of importance to the malaria modelling community including precipitation, near surface air temperatures and winds (and variables which can be used to calculate relative humidity). The spatial resolution is relatively course (0.25°), but it provides data at very high temporal resolution (hourly) and the dataset is updated daily with just a 5 day latency.

  • ERA5-Land hourly from 1950 to present ERA5-Land is a enhanced spatial resolution version of ERA5 providing data at 9km. It provides a large range of climatic, surface, vegetation and other variables. The dataset is updated daily with just a 5 day latency.

Seasonal forecasts

  • Seasonal forecast daily and subdaily data on single levels This catalogue provides a multi-system seasonal forecast service. CDS collects, processes and combines the output from several seasonal forecast models and makes them available of user-relevant applications. Forecasts of key variables such as temperature and precipitation are available for retrospective and future seasonal analysis

Climate projections

  • CMIP6 Climate projections This catalogue provides daily and monthly global climate model projections data from the sixth phase of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP6). A range of variables are provides from a large number of models covering both the historical and future period represented by eight future Shared Socioenomic Pathway (SSP) scenarios.

Strengths

The Climate Data Store aims to be a one-stop shop for climate data and analysis. It hosts a very large number of key datasets and provides detailed guidance information, training and tools.

Limitations

The CDS is large and can be confusing and intimidating. The focus is on Europe and not southern Africa.

How can this data be used in disease modelling?

Using a country-wide, gridded reanalysis product like the Copernicus Climate Data Store (CDS) ERA5 dataset transforms national disease modeling by providing continuous, gap-free environmental drivers across the entirety of Nigeria. Unlike scattered point-source station observations, reanalysis data blends satellite and in-situ observations into numerical weather models, offering a complete historical archive of multi-variable climate fields at a national scale.

To explicitly integrate these environmental conditions into a national architecture, we expand the mechanistic equations adapted from Mordecai et al. (2013) and Agusto (2020) by utilizing a spatially distributed ensemble framework. Rather than calculating a simplified, single national average of weather parameters which commits an ecological fallacy due to the highly non-linear curves governing vector biology we resolve the temperature-dependent human biting rates (a), parasite development rates (gamma_m), daily oviposition rates (ovi), and humidity-driven desiccation mortality (mu_m) at the individual grid-cell level before evaluating the national ordinary differential equation (ODE) system. This ensures that localized climate extremes, such as dry Harmattan dust lanes in the north or humid coastal monsoons in the south, are biologically preserved rather than mathematically smoothed away.

Preparing the data

Using the national hourly ERA5 single-levels dataset, we pull t2m (temperature in Kelvin), d2m (dewpoint in Kelvin), and tp (total precipitation in meters). We converted the thermodynamic metrics into Celsius and compute relative humidity via the Magnus-Tetens formula to map country-wide transmission vectors over time. The key modelling shift here is that we apply the non-linear biological trait equations to each grid cell prior to national summation, yielding an ecologically accurate set of daily forcing arrays from 2010 to 2025.

There are different approaches to explicitly include environmental conditions into an infectious disease model. Specifically, the example below describes temperature-dependent human biting rates, mosquito mortality, and the parasite’s Extrinsic Incubation Period (EIP). It also includes a rainfall-dependent environmental carrying capacity that accounts for hydrological extremes, specifically the physical disruption or “flushing” of aquatic larval habitats during heavy rainfall events exceeding 30 mm.

Show the code 
# Load and process the gridded CDS ERA5 data
era5_grid_processed <- read_csv("era5_hourly_nigeria.csv") |> 
  mutate(
    DATE = as_date(valid_time),
    temp_C = t2m - 273.15,
    dew_C = d2m - 273.15,
    # Derive Relative Humidity per grid cell via vapor pressures
    sat_vap_press = 0.6108 * exp((17.27 * temp_C) / (237.7 + temp_C)),
    act_vap_press = 0.6108 * exp((17.27 * dew_C) / (237.7 + dew_C)),
    RH = (act_vap_press / sat_vap_press) * 100,
    # Handle any potential NA values in total precipitation before conversion
    tp_clean = ifelse(is.na(tp), 0, tp),
    PRCP_mm = tp_clean * 1000
  ) |> 
  # Step 2: Apply non-linear biological equations AT THE LOCAL GRID LEVEL using vectorized pmax
  mutate(
    cell_a = pmax(-0.00014 * temp_C^2 + 0.027 * temp_C - 0.322, 0.53),
    cell_gamma_m = pmax(0.000112 * temp_C * (temp_C - 15.38) * sqrt(pmax(0, 35 - temp_C)), 0),
    cell_ovi = pmax(-0.153 * temp_C^2 + 8.61 * temp_C - 97.7, 0),
        
    # Combined local thermal limit + relative humidity desiccation stress
    mu_thermal = -log(pmax(-0.000828 * temp_C^2 + 0.0367 * temp_C + 0.522, 0.01)),
    mu_desicc  = ifelse(RH < 55, 0.04 * exp((55 - RH) * 0.07), 0),
    cell_mu_m  = mu_thermal + mu_desicc,
        
    # Local aquatic larval habitat carrying capacity (incorporating local flushing > 30mm)
    cell_K_factor = ifelse(PRCP_mm > 30, PRCP_mm * 0.3, PRCP_mm + 1)
  ) |> 
  # Step 3: Aggregate responses across all coordinates to form coherent national drivers
  group_by(DATE) |> 
  summarise(
    nat_a       = mean(cell_a, na.rm = TRUE),
    nat_gamma_m = mean(cell_gamma_m, na.rm = TRUE),
    nat_ovi     = mean(cell_ovi, na.rm = TRUE),
    nat_mu_m    = mean(cell_mu_m, na.rm = TRUE),
    nat_K_scale = mean(cell_K_factor, na.rm = TRUE),
    # Keep track of raw aggregated metrics for visual verification and tracking
    TAVG        = mean(temp_C, na.rm = TRUE),
    PRCP        = mean(PRCP_mm, na.rm = TRUE), # Fixed: Use mean to avoid multiplying rainfall across grid totals
    RH_AVG      = mean(RH, na.rm = TRUE),
    .groups = "drop"
  ) |> 
  filter(DATE >= ymd("2010-01-01") & DATE <= ymd("2025-08-23")) |> 
  arrange(DATE)

We incorporate pre-resolved, distributed biological parameters directly into our national mathematical model. While other frameworks treat localised metrics as unlinked variables, this model explicitly integrates humidity as a non-linear driver of adult vector desiccation stress within the mortality equation (mu_m).

Furthermore, geographic altitude is natively captured by the ERA5 reanalysis fields, as the underlying 2-meter temperature (t2m) profiles inherently adjust for topographic elevation via the environmental lapse rate. For this country-wide analysis, we explore national mosquito populations and disease prevalence under the influence of these overlapping environmental forces.

We achieve this by holding baseline epidemiological parameters and initial host population structures constant, allowing the shifting, multi-variable climate fields of temperature, precipitation, and relative humidity to dynamically drive transmission potential across the country.

# Simulation Timeline Setup
Y = 10
times <- seq(0, 365 * Y, by = 1)

# Extraction of aligned biological forcing arrays
forced_a       <- era5_grid_processed |> fill(nat_a, .direction = "downup") |> pull(nat_a)
forced_gamma_m <- era5_grid_processed |> fill(nat_gamma_m, .direction = "downup") |> pull(nat_gamma_m)
forced_ovi     <- era5_grid_processed |> fill(nat_ovi, .direction = "downup") |> pull(nat_ovi)
forced_mu_m    <- era5_grid_processed |> fill(nat_mu_m, .direction = "downup") |> pull(nat_mu_m)
forced_K_scale <- era5_grid_processed |> fill(nat_K_scale, .direction = "downup") |> pull(nat_K_scale)

# Spatially Corrected SEACR-SEI Model Engine
seacr_era5_ensemble <- function(times, start, parameters, a_vec, gamma_m_vec, ovi_vec, mu_m_vec, K_vec) {
  with(as.list(c(start, parameters)), {
    P = S + E + A + C + R + G
    M = Sm + Em + Im
    
    t_day <- min(floor(times) + 1, length(a_vec))
    
    # Pull pre-resolved biological parameters for day t
    a       <- a_vec[t_day]
    gamma_m <- gamma_m_vec[t_day]
    ovi     <- ovi_vec[t_day]
    mu_m    <- mu_m_vec[t_day]
    K       <- Km * K_vec[t_day]
    
    # Transmission dynamics based on aggregated local suitability
    Infectious <- C + zeta_a * A
    lambda.v   <- a * (M / P) * b * (Im / M)
    lambda.h   <- a * c * (Infectious / P)
    
    # System Differentials
    dSm  <- ovi * (1 - M / K) * M - lambda.h * Sm - mu_m * Sm
    dEm  <- lambda.h * Sm - (gamma_m + mu_m) * Em
    dIm  <- gamma_m * Em - mu_m * Im
    
    dS   <- mu_h * P - lambda.v * S + rho * R - mu_h * S
    dE   <- lambda.v * S - (gamma_h + mu_h) * E
    dA   <- pa * gamma_h * E + pa * gamma_h * G - (delta + mu_h) * A
    dC   <- (1 - pa) * gamma_h * E + (1 - pa) * gamma_h * G - (r + mu_h) * C
    dR   <- delta * A + r * C - (lambda.v + rho + mu_h) * R
    dG   <- lambda.v * R - (gamma_h + mu_h) * G
    dCInc <- lambda.v * (S + R)
    
    list(c(dSm, dEm, dIm, dS, dE, dA, dC, dR, dG, dCInc))
  })
}

# Complete system initialization
initial_state <- c(Sm = 70000, Em = 25000, Im = 35000, S = 85000, E = 15000, A = 8000, C = 6000, R = 4000, G = 1000, CInc = 0)
parameters    <- c(b = 0.72, c = 0.52, r = 1/21, rho = 1/150, delta = 1/140, zeta_a = 0.4, pa = 0.38, mu_h = 1/(60 * 365), gamma_h = 1/10, Km = 45000)

# Run the spatially corrected system
out_Nigeria <- ode(y = initial_state, times = times, func = seacr_era5_ensemble, parms = parameters,
                   a_vec = forced_a, gamma_m_vec = forced_gamma_m, ovi_vec = forced_ovi, mu_m_vec = forced_mu_m, K_vec = forced_K_scale)

df_nigeria_results <- as_tibble(as.data.frame(out_Nigeria)) |> 
  mutate(P = S + E + A + C + R + G,
         M = Sm + Em + Im,
         Inc = c(0, diff(CInc)),
         date = ymd("2010-01-01") + time, 
         Country = "Nigeria"
  ) |>
  pivot_longer(cols = c(-time, -date, -Country), names_to = "variable", values_to = "value")

Changes in vector population

Vector population dynamics across Nigeria follow a highly synchronized seasonal curve when simulated under a distributed ensemble logic. The human population remains constant, and secondary infections occur when individuals are re-exposed to infectious vectors while recovering from a primary infection channel.

The interactions between local environmental stressors create distinct macro-trends in the mosquito population (M). During the dry season, low relative humidity values driven by the regional Harmattan wind patterns induce heavy desiccation stress across northern grids, spiking vector mortality (mu_m) and causing a substantial drop in the overall national vector population. When the seasonal monsoon rains arrive, this atmospheric pressure eases and expanding aquatic breeding habitats across all grid coordinates trigger a rapid, widespread resurgence in mosquito populations across the country.

Implications for transmission

The national force of infection is driven by human biting frequencies, the ratio of active vectors to human hosts, transmission probabilities, and the scale of the infectious human reservoir (C + zeta_a A). By processing climate fields on a distributed mesh, our equations explicitly capture transmission bottlenecks that simple aggregated models tend to miss.

The resulting simulation demonstrates an intense, perennial transmission profile that is bounded by seasonal transitions. During dry-season intervals, transmission slows considerably; even though ambient temperatures remain high enough to support rapid parasite development (EIP), the low relative humidity shortens adult mosquito lifespans, meaning few vectors survive long enough to become fully infectious (Im). Once the wet season breaks, the pairing of optimal temperatures and elevated relative humidity extends vector longevity, unleashing high-velocity transmission cycles that produce distinct, broad annual waves in clinical cases (C) across the population.

Note that these outputs represent illustrative indicators of transmission potential derived from multi-variable environmental suitability. In operational contexts, recorded clinical case data may deviate from these metrics due to healthcare access limits, localized interventions, or diagnostic availability.

Policy implications

Models that evaluate non-linear temperature, rainfall, and relative humidity matrices on a distributed grid enable the National Malaria Elimination Programme (NMEP) of Nigeria to transition from reactive management to proactive, forecast-driven public health planning:

Spatially Optimized National Interventions: Because this framework captures how relative humidity acts as a key survival bottleneck for vectors, control programs can time the distribution of Long-Lasting Insecticidal Nets (LLINs) and seasonal chemoprevention to conclude right before the national humidity upswing, targeting the vector population at its most vulnerable point.

Proactive Supply Chain Routing for Seasonal Surges: Anticipating the predictable, climate-driven expansion of clinical cases late in the wet season allows health ministries to build proactive supply lines. Rapid Diagnostic Tests (RDTs) and Artemisin-based Combination Therapies (ACTs) can be moved into regional distribution hubs ahead of the surge, preventing stockouts during high-transmission months.

Evaluating Interannual Climate Resilience: Incorporating continuous Copernicus Climate Data Store feeds helps health authorities identify anomalous years such as prolonged rainy seasons or unseasonably humid dry intervals allowing teams to adjust national control budgets and operational timelines dynamically.
